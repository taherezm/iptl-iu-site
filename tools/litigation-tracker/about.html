Litigation Tracker

What the tracker is doing.

The tracker follows federal cases where technology is forcing old legal categories to do new work: AI model training, generated output, software patents, scraping, platform disputes, licensing, trade secrets, right of publicity, and other fights where the technical facts actually matter.

Back to tracker
Method

It is a research tool, not a scoreboard.

Most public legal coverage starts when a complaint is filed or when a judge writes something worth quoting. We are trying to watch the quieter middle: docket entries, motion practice, scheduling orders, consolidation, discovery fights, dismissals, appeals, and the moments where a broad technology story turns into an actual litigation record.

The point is not to declare who is right. A party filing something is not the same thing as a court agreeing with it. The tracker is built to preserve that line.

What gets tracked.

  • Copyright cases involving training data, model outputs, scraped works, authors, publishers, music, images, code, or media archives.
  • Patent disputes involving software, machine learning systems, voice tools, search, recommendation, data processing, and related technical products.
  • Trade secret, DMCA, trademark, right-of-publicity, biometric, privacy, open-source, and platform disputes when the intellectual property or technical system is central rather than incidental.

How the data moves.

The pipeline searches CourtListener, screens candidate cases for AI and IP relevance, pulls docket activity for active matters, summarizes new docket entries, and publishes normalized JSON that the tracker page reads directly. Source links point back to CourtListener because the source of record is still the docket, not this site.

Automated summaries are kept narrow. They describe procedural movement, not ultimate liability, unless the docket entry itself reports a ruling. We validate the data before publication and flag cases for review when a docket entry looks significant or case-ending.

What it is not.

This is not legal advice, a substitute for PACER, or a claim that every relevant case has been captured. It is a working public index for students, researchers, and anyone else trying to follow how AI, software, platforms, and intellectual property are being argued in court.

If a case is missing or a summary looks off, write to undergradtechlaw@outlook.com.
